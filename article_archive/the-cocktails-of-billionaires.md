For Musk, it’s as if he has a winning lottery ticket worth billions, but he must cash it in before the expiration date.

The problem is that if Elon Musk sells a significant portion of his stock now, then it will send the message to Wall Street that he believes that Tesla has reached the peak of its value. The result would undoubtedly be a selloff from other investors that would result in the market price of Tesla shares coming down, which would fuel the already growing interest in short-selling of the overvalued automaker.

In the end, this would all significantly lower the overall value of Musk’s remaining stake in the company, and piss off his major investors.

In other words, Musk is sitting on an incredible fortune, one that by all traditional standards of business, economics, and moral decency, he did not earn. If he tries to claim it, it might jeopardize his ability to take more in the future.

Musk suggested that this was in response to recent calls by politicians to tax unrealized gains — it’s not, it’s a clever way for him to cash in on over $20 billion before it’s too late.

The fact that Tesla stock is above $1,200 per share is in itself ridiculous. As of the publishing of this article, the market value of the storied automaker is exponentially higher than what the company is actually worth — this is based upon the generally accepted principles of accounting, economics, finance, and math. Perhaps the most glaring of these principles is that the current Market Cap of Tesla is $1.23 Trillion, meanwhile, the total assets of Tesla are around $58 billion — that’s just 5% of what the automaker is currently valued at. Additionally, the Price-Earnings Ratio (P/E) is around 395, which is more than 40x the average for the automotive industry.

Under normal circumstances, the stock price of Tesla would be falling back to earth faster than a defective SpaceX rocket. However, thanks largely to the cult of personality around Elon Musk, the price of Tesla remains where it is.

If Musk unloads 10% of his shares now, he will receive roughly $20 billion from the sale, and while, yes, he will pay taxes on that, the cost of doing so will be way less than losing out on a chance to cash in on the fact that Tesla is so overvalued.

But again, he needed a clever reason to do so, so he once again went to Twitter to manipulate the market, and come up with a casus belli to go from an unrealized multi-billionaire, to an actual one.

Now, before all the Musk zealots start attacking me and saying that Tesla is not overvalued, let’s go over the major reasons why Tesla is definitely not worth anywhere near $1.23 trillion.

### The many lies of Tesla

Tesla became valuable by convincing people they were valuable — it’s that simple. Furthermore, Elon Musk lied to investors.

On January 29, 2020, [Musk said during an earnings call that](https://www.cnbc.com/2020/01/29/elon-musk-tesla-wont-raise-capital-will-focus-on-battery-cost.html):

> “We’re spending money as quickly as we can spend it sensibly — We are not artificially limiting our progress. Despite all that, we are still generating positive cash. In light of that, it doesn’t make sense to raise money because we expect to generate cash despite this growth level.”

In other words, Musk told investors that although shares of Tesla were skyrocketing, the company would not raise additional capital via a public offering of stock.

One year later, Tesla had gone through not one, but three new public offerings, raising a total of $12.6 billion.

Just by themselves, these public offerings increased the total assets of Tesla by almost 30%, which is a major reason why the current market value of the company is not much more than 20x total assets (which in itself is obscene).

Tesla was able to convince some investors that Tesla was worth way more than it actually was by making outrageous and false claims including that Tesla would soon have a self-driving vehicle.

Elon Musk is, for several reasons, able to tells lies without anyone holding him accountable. The latest example of this is the Hertz partnership.

On Twitter, Musk announced that Hertz had agreed to purchase 100,000 Tesla vehicles — the result was that the stock price of Tesla went up, yet again. In reality, however, there was no agreement, and the sale might never actually happen. Even if it does, it will almost certainly not be as lucrative and amazing as Elon Musk made it sound.

It didn’t matter, the stock price had already skyrocketed based upon the false information.

Musk has been masterful at using the democratization of the investment world to convince enough people that Tesla will become the most valuable automaker in the world, and so, those people in turn have made it the most valuable automaker in the world, despite that the fundamentals are simply not there.

As of now, Tesla is a house of cards on a very windy day.

### Tesla is not a tech company

Another thing that Musk has been masterful at is convincing people that Tesla is a tech giant, when in reality, it is relatively small automotive company. Why is that important? When it comes to the automotive world, investors tend to be much more conservative and focused on the long-term, and things like fundamentals matter. In the tech industry, it’s the exact opposite, and promises of the future are everything.

Tesla never would never have been able to operate as it has under the financial norms of the automotive industry, and so it’s been important that Musk continue to attract more liberal and tech minded investors.

In reality, Tesla doesn’t look like a tech company, it looks like an automaker, because it makes cars. Just because the company owns the patent to a battery technology doesn’t automatically make it a “technological innovator.” The fact that Tesla owns invention patents for something that must be manufactured, actually makes it look more like a traditional automaker than a typical tech company, which tend to rely heavily on intellectual property and intangibles such as software.

### Tesla is not profitable

During the entire 17 year existence of the EV automaker, Tesla has never been profitable from operations. The first time the company actually turned any profit whatsoever was in 2020, after 16 years of operations. While many celebrated this fact as proof that Tesla was the way of the future, the reality is that Tesla did not earn this profit from selling cars.

To be clear, Tesla did, in fact, [report a profit for 2020](https://www.fool.com/investing/2021/01/27/tesla-earnings-5-must-see-metrics-highlight-strong/?utm_source=nasdaq&utm_medium=feed&utm_campaign=article&referring_guid=1ca925f9-9f82-4d9d-b3d8-aef16ecbffeb), however, that profit came from the $1.58 billion of revenue that the company earned by [selling regulatory credits](https://www.fool.com/investing/2020/08/29/tesla-the-curious-case-of-the-vanishing-sentence-t/?utm_source=nasdaq&utm_medium=feed&utm_campaign=article&referring_guid=1ca925f9-9f82-4d9d-b3d8-aef16ecbffeb). Tesla’s net income of $721 million in 2020 turns into a substantial loss if those regulatory credit sales are not considered.

What are regulatory credits?

In America, many U.S. states award regulatory credits to automakers for selling electric vehicles. Automakers must acquire a minimum number of these credits to comply with regulatory requirements. These credits can be bought and sold, so an automaker that doesn’t sell enough electric vehicles can buy credits from other automakers that do.

Tesla has a surplus of these credits relative to the minimum required to meet government emissions standards — this is because they are, in fact, an automotive company. Tesla has thus sold these surplus credits to other automakers, such as Toyota or General Motors, whose limited electric vehicle production means that they wouldn’t otherwise achieve compliance with regulatory standards.

In other words, Tesla became profitable by selling regulatory compliance to other automakers who produce gas guzzling vehicles.

### Tesla scammed their way into the S&P 500 at the expense of conservative investors

Prior to 2020, Tesla was not listed in the S&P 500 because the index requires that a company actually turn a profit in order to be included. Despite that the profit in 2020 was not actually from normal operations, that the company posted a gain, forced S&P to include it on the list of global business giants.

However, Tesla’s entry meant another company had to leave. To make room, S&P kicked out Apartment Investment and Management, a company whose stock spiked almost 60% since leaving — this would have been better for investors who purchase the index than having Tesla included in the S&P.

### Tesla is not why the world is adopting Electric Vehicles

One thing that many supporters and followers of Elon Musk love to claim is that he is single handedly forcing the Electric Vehicle revolution in order to save the world from Climate Change catastrophe.

This isn’t even close to true.

What is pushing the expansion of the electric vehicle market is investments from major governments around the world, particularly China, the European Union, and yes, the USA. This includes upgrades to power grids and other electrical infrastructure that need to become more renewable in order for EV’s to make environmental sense.

The regulatory credit system mentioned above is an attempt by governments to encourage electric vehicle production and reduce emissions — it is by accident that it ended up subsidizing Tesla’s money-losing automobile operations.

Of course, this situation is not going to last forever, as other automakers are aggressively ramping up their electric vehicle efforts.

In other words, there is no reason to believe that Tesla has any real advantage in the EV market beyond that Elon Musk says so.

General Motors, for example, is [investing billions into its electric vehicle efforts](https://www.fool.com/investing/2020/11/19/general-motors-goes-even-bigger-on-electric-vehicl/?utm_source=nasdaq&utm_medium=feed&utm_campaign=article&referring_guid=1ca925f9-9f82-4d9d-b3d8-aef16ecbffeb), as the Detroit-based automaker has plans to launch dozens of new EV models within the next few years. GM also has [a new commercial electric vehicle brand](https://www.fool.com/investing/2021/01/12/general-motors-challenges-ford-and-workhorse-with/?utm_source=nasdaq&utm_medium=feed&utm_campaign=article&referring_guid=1ca925f9-9f82-4d9d-b3d8-aef16ecbffeb), which already has a contract with FedEx.

Unlike Tesla’s contract with Hertz, this contract between GM and FedEx is actually real.
