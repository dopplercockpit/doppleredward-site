Despite what many want to believe, there is only one way for the western world to meet climate, energy, and security goals, and that’s nuclear power. Nuclear power plants provide an incredible amount of energy, and they do it without any carbon emissions or harmful particulates that contaminate the air that all of us breathe. Based on existing resources, nuclear power plants are sustainable for at least the [next three centuries](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/how-long-will-global-uranium-deposits-last/), however, new exploration, advancements in technology, and improvements in extraction methods are predicted to [at least double this estimate over the next couple of decades](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/how-long-will-global-uranium-deposits-last/), but only if the required economic investment is available.

Unfortunately, anti-nuclear activists around the world have spent years fomenting fear about atomic power, and many people have bought into it. As a result, lawmakers throughout the western world have been pressured to close nuclear plants that were previously producing clean, reliable, and safe energy.

While anti-nuclear advocates have insisted that renewables such as solar and wind can replace nuclear, the reality of rolling back the supply of nuclear power has instead been an increased reliance on carbon-emitting fossil fuels. This is incredibly stupid because the only way to stop climate change is to greatly reduce the consumption of fossil fuels. Additionally, the nations that control much of the remaining supply of oil and gas, are nations such as Russia and Saudi Arabia, who are far from being friendly to the western world.

Nuclear power is likely the only way for us to continue to meet our energy demands while at the same time eliminating carbon emissions that are causing climate change, greatly reducing other forms of air pollution that cause hundreds of thousands of deaths each year, and establishing energy independence for free democracies around the world.

Denying this fact will undoubtedly lead the world to disaster.

### The German problem

One great example of a spectacular clusterbomb of stupidity regarding nuclear energy policy can be seen in Germany, where policy leaders have entirely dismantled that country’s nuclear power grid.

After the 2011 [Fukushima disaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fukushima_nuclear_disaster), where a Japanese nuclear power plant experienced multiple meltdowns as a result of a major earthquake and subsequent tsunami, German public opinion of nuclear power plummeted around the world. Despite the fact that most top energy experts warned that nuclear phaseouts would crunch the energy supply at a time when countries were shifting away from fossil fuels in order to meet climate mandates, and that a resulting overreliance on oil and gas would further compromise national security, the German government stubbornly moved forward with shutting down 14 of its 17 nuclear plants, with the final three slated to be turned off by the end of 2022.

Of course, this quickly proved to be a terrible idea since Germany eliminated one pretend threat and traded it for a very real one — Russia.

Today, Germany is stuck in a bad situation where it must support the Ukrainians and NATO sanctions against Russia on one hand, yet work with Russia for fuel imports on the other. Furthermore, Germany has not been able to live up to the promises of establishing an entirely renewable power grid, as a significant portion of its energy is still being generated by fossil fuels.

### About face

The war in Ukraine has been a game changer for nuclear, as it has led even the most fervent nuclear critics to face the reality that reducing domestic nuclear energy production will result in a reliance on Russian fossil fuels, which of course is no longer a possibility for many. In fact, the [European Union recently decided](https://www.wsj.com/articles/gas-and-nuclear-power-can-be-green-under-new-eu-plan-11657108882?mod=article_inline) to classify nuclear energy as “green,” which potentially opens up billions of euros in investment that were previously unavailable for advancing the technology of atom splitting.

EU member states are also beginning to act on their own. For example, Belgium’s Green Party recently made a similar adjustment to their belief system when they extended the life of their country’s remaining two reactors by a whopping ten years. Poland, which was on the opposite end of the spectrum and had heavily invested in coal, is building its first nuclear plant, while the neighbouring Czech Republic also plans to construct four new reactors over the next several years.

France, a country where 70% of its energy comes from nuclear, was also headed down the path of denuclearization and planned on shuttering 14 of its 58 reactors. That has quickly changed, however, as the French are now doubling down on nuclear energy, including next-generation designs. It would seem that France has no interest in winding up in a similar situation as their German neighbours.

Ironically, Germany may soon find itself needing to purchase nuclear-generated energy from France, as relations with Russia continue to sour.

To the north, the Dutch government is planning on building two new plants, and they have even gone so far as to plead with Germany to keep its remaining three nuclear plants online.

The USA, which has closed 12 reactors since 2012, is now allocating [$6 billion in aid](https://www.energy.gov/articles/doe-establishes-6-billion-program-preserve-americas-clean-nuclear-energy-infrastructure) to some of its struggling nuclear plants. Meanwhile, private investors and various government agencies are pouring billions of dollars into next-generation nuclear designs, while individual states are repealing long-standing bans on nuclear power. Wyoming recently convinced Bill Gates to build his [first fast reactor there](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TerraPower), while NuScale, the company that created the first approved commercial small modular reactor design, is building several next-generation reactors in Idaho.

The story is similar in Asia and the Pacific.

Japan plans to reverse its denuclearization policy and is planning on bringing nine new nuclear reactors fully online this winter. Additionally, its 2030 carbon emissions goal is based on restarting a total of 30 dormant reactors.

South Korea recently announced its goal to boost nuclear generation by 30% over eight years, while Indonesia, Vietnam, and the Philippines are all investing heavily in new nuclear energy. Indonesia in particular has passed legislation to begin building its first nuclear power station.

Even China is in the nuclear game, as they have now established themselves as the world’s single largest investor in new nuclear energy projects.

### History repeats itself

Despite what is obvious to much of the rest of the world, Germany remains stubborn. Perhaps it is pride, or perhaps they are just that foolish — whatever the reason, the German government remains on pace to decommission all remaining plants by the end of this year.

German Economy and Climate Minister Robert Habeck [said that](https://abcnews.go.com/International/wireStory/correction-germany-nuclear-shutdown-story-82051054), “by massively increasing renewable energy and accelerating the expansion of the electricity grid we can show that this [non-nuclear energy] is possible in Germany,” while Environment Minister Steffi Lemke [declared that](https://abcnews.go.com/International/wireStory/correction-germany-nuclear-shutdown-story-82051054), “Nuclear power plants remain high-risk facilities that produce highly radioactive atomic waste.”

> “Never underestimate the power of stupid people in large groups” — George Carlin

The truth in Germany is that renewable energy sources delivered only 46% of the electricity generated in 2021, while fossil fuels accounted for over 41%. Meanwhile, the remaining three nuclear power reactors, the very same that Germany plans on closing later this year, provide over 13% of total power.

Given that there are no fast renewable options that can be implemented, the Germans will likely find themselves a majority fossil fuel country once again, one that depends upon a hostile Russia for oil.

It is indeed ironic that the man responsible for discovering how to harness the power of the atom was a German who fled his homeland because the government of his day would have persecuted him for being Jewish.

That man was Albert Einstein.

Today, the Germans are once again persecuting nuclear and driving out potential innovators for no good reason other than misguided fears of a fake threat, and once again, they will pay the price for placing baseless ideology over ethics, science, and common sense.

While the proud denizens of the Deutschlands might prefer an unfriendly alliance with war-mongering authoritarians versus admitting their folly over giving up on clean and safe nuclear energy, the rest of the world should take note of Berlin’s mega mistake, and get on board with the power of the atom.

### So how many people have actually died as a result of nuclear power?

Over the roughly 70 years that nuclear power has existed, there have been a total of 667 nuclear power plants that have ever operated, and only three major accidents have taken place. Using the [official internationally-recognized death statistics for those three disasters](https://www.iea.org/reports/key-world-energy-statistics-2020) that occurred at Three Mile Island, Chernobyl, and Fukushima, the combined loss of lives from the three major incidents is 32 people.

By this estimate, the number of deaths caused by the nuclear energy sector overall is the least of any energy sector.

For comparison, one hydroelectric dam failure killed 26,000 people in China in 1975, another killed 75 people in Russia in 2009, and in 2017, 180,000 people had to suddenly evacuate their homes in Northern California as a result of a damaged spillway that threatened to flood the surrounding area.

Regarding solar and wind, every year hundreds of people die as a result of electrocution, falling accidents, and other hazards that can occur with the construction and installation of solar panels and wind turbines.

Regardless, renewables and green energy are not the real culprits for deaths; that honour rests firmly with fossil fuel energy. The truth is that turning away from nuclear almost always means a heavier reliance on fossil fuels, which in turn claims the lives of hundreds of thousands of people every single year.

Climate change alone is believed to cause countless deaths annually, but many deaths also occur during mining, drilling and construction — this is to say nothing of the impact on respiratory and cardiovascular health. [By one estimate](https://www.engineering.com/story/whats-the-death-toll-of-nuclear-vs-other-energy-sources), the loss of life from fossil fuels is estimated at 100,000 deaths for every 1000TWh of energy supplied by burning coal, 36,000 deaths for oil, and 4000 deaths for natural gas.

To give some perspective, the world consumes 170,000TWh per year, and in 2020, oil supplied 32% of the world’s total energy, while coal supplied 27% and natural gas came in just shy of 23%.

### The Fukushima nuclear disaster was easily avoidable

What happened in Fukushima was the result of an [extremely rare 9.1 magnitude megathrust earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_T%C5%8Dhoku_earthquake_and_tsunami) that triggered an incredibly powerful tsunami with waves as high as 45 meters (133 feet) that directly struck the Fukushima Daiichi Nuclear Power Plant that had been foolishly built along the earthquake-prone coastline of the Pacific Ocean.

The earthquake was the most powerful ever recorded in Japan’s history, and the fourth most powerful in the entire world since seismic recording began in 1900. Damage from the resulting enormous tsunami was actually even worse, with powerful surges utterly destroying entire towns as far inland as 10km (6.2 miles) from the coast.

Regardless of how extreme and rare the natural disaster that caused the nuclear disaster actually was, there were, indeed, many warnings prior to 2011 about the safety of the power plants in Fukushima.

In 1990, the U.S. [Nuclear Regulatory Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_Regulatory_Commission) (NRC) declared that the failure of emergency electricity generators, and the subsequent loss of vital cooling systems for reactors, was of significant risk for nuclear plants located in seismically active regions; this was said to be one of the most likely risks for causing a meltdown. The Japanese [Nuclear and Industrial Safety Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_and_Industrial_Safety_Agency) (NISA) actually cited this American report in 2004, and sent it to both the Japanese government and the power company that owned the power facilities in Fukushima — [no action was taken](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2011-03-16/japan-s-reactor-risk-foretold-20-years-ago-in-u-s-nuclear-agency-s-report).

Additionally, [Tokyo Electric Power Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Electric_Power_Company) (TEPCO), which operated the plants at Fukushima, was warned by engineers that their seawalls were insufficient to withstand a powerful tsunami — again, nothing was done.

Despite ignoring warnings and suffering a direct hit from one of the worst natural disasters of modern history, the nuclear disaster at Fukushima wasn’t nearly as bad as it could have been. In the end, only a single person died from radiation, and only eighteen more were injured severely — this is far from the fear-mongering claims of antinuclear activism that warn of entire cities being wiped out by future accidents at nuclear power plants.

More telling perhaps is the fact that there was another nuclear power plant, Fukushima Daini, just 12km (7.5 miles) south of the Fukushima Daiichi Nuclear Power Plant, which is where the disaster happened. At Fukushima Daini, no meltdowns, explosions, or radiation leaks occurred whatsoever. Following the earthquake and Tsunami, the four reactors at Fukushima Daini suffered extensive damage, yet the reactors [automatically shut down](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scram) and the plant was back under control within two days.

What happened at Daiichi and Daini is actually a testament to how safe nuclear power plants actually are, even when they are built along the Pacific coastline just over tectonic plate fault lines that are capable of unleashing megathrust earthquakes of biblical proportions.

Rather than look to what happened in Fukushima as a reason to eliminate nuclear altogether, the better approach would be to construct nuclear plants in safer locations.

### The way through is forward not backwards

Existing nuclear energy relies on a process called fission, where a heavy chemical element is split to produce lighter ones. It is these types of reactions that cause heavy amounts of radiation that can poison, well, everything.

Nuclear fusion, on the other hand, is a process that works by combining two light elements to make a heavier one, and thus there is no significant radiation generated.

Scientists already know how to create fusion reactions; the problem is figuring out how to get more energy out of a fusion reaction than is required to start the process in the first place (aka the catalyst).

There is good news though.

Investment in nuclear technologies has allowed several labs around the world to discover new ways to harness nuclear fusion on Earth, in a way that would be sustainable, since the power generated from these experiments is rapidly nearing what is required to surpass the requirements for catalysts.

This is super exciting since fusion is the same process that powers the stars, including our own sun. The result of controlled fusion on Earth would be a virtually unlimited supply of low-carbon, low-radiation energy that produces almost zero hazardous waste.

Simply put, nuclear fusion is the game-changing technology that would move us from an advanced industrial society to one that more closely resembles [Star Trek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_Trek).

Despite the incredible and very attainable promises of advanced nuclear technologies, fear of disaster has caused many to turn away and instead look to the incredibly unrealistic and unsustainable technologies of solar, wind, and geothermal, none of which generate nearly enough power to meet growing global demands.

These fears are unfounded and short-sighted, and they will kill millions of people if we don’t all get real very soon.
