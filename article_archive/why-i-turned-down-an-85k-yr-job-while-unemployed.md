If you pose the question:

> ***“Where do people go to invest?”***

Most people would respond by saying,

> ***“The stock market.”***

While this is by far the most common response, it is, in fact, quite wrong.

All around the world, investing is carried out using a wide variety of financial instruments that are primarily traded on things called **exchanges.**

How many exchanges are there? A lot, but more on that later.

When people say “*The Stock Market*,” they are oftentimes incorrectly referring to the global financial system where people trade all types of things including money, metals, fruits, contracts, and pieces of companies.

Pieces of companies? Yes, and that is what people in the English-speaking world are talking about when they say **stocks.**

So where are stocks traded? On the Stock Market of course! And that is the only thing traded there. But what is that exactly?

## Taking Stock of Stocks

When someone buys a single stock, they are purchasing a small portion of a publicly traded corporation, aka, a company.

What does that mean “publicly traded?”

Well, simply put, it means that anyone with a little bit of money can purchase a portion of a company. If a company is private, then a person needs permission of the owners, regardless of how much money they have.

For example, if you have an extra $1,000, then you can easily go online and purchase a couple of shares in your favorite major corporations, as long as they are sold on a **stock exchange**. However, if you want to buy shares in the local café down the street, you can’t do it without permission from the owner, unless that café happens to be a Starbucks (SBUX).

Now, you have probably heard of the term **shares** being used to describe stocks — well that’s because that’s what stocks represent! Each single little stock represents a portion, or *share*, of a company.

So, when it comes to publicly traded companies, the ownership of those companies is quite literally being *shared* amongst everyone who possesses stocks, or shares, of that company.

Make sense?

If this sounds absurd, it’s probably because you, or someone you know, owned 10 shares of a major company such as Apple, Wal-Mart, General Electric, etc… and certainly, there were no invites to any owner meetings. Be that as it may, while perhaps there were no premium raw oysters aboard the corporate jet, owning 10 shares in any company means that, technically and legally, that a person holding shares is an *owner* of that company.

Now, if this proud owner of 10 shares was you, the reason why you didn’t get any special treatment is because there are literally hundreds of thousands, or perhaps even millions, of owners, and they won’t all fit on a private jet.

Major corporations issue millions or even billions of shares to the public, so having 10, 100, 1000, or even 10,000 shares is oftentimes not even a drop in a giant rain barrel!

As an example, Apple (APPL), the storied maker of the infamous iPhone, is one of the largest companies in the world. Currently, Apple has more than 16 billion shares outstanding!

### What’s The Point Of Buying Stocks Then?

So if we can’t get the corporate perks or have a say in how the company is managed, why buy stocks?

Well… because:

> ***Owning shares in a company is a way to own the future value of that company.***

In other words, if the company performs well is thus worth more than when you originally purchased it, then the portion of the company that you own increases in value! If one year ago you put $1000 towards purchasing 10 shares at $100 each, but today each share is worth $120, then the value of your share has increased by $200! If you decided to go and sell your shares to someone else, then you would profit by that amount.

Also, owners of stocks (this depends but let’s keep it simple for now) typically get to vote on important matters such as elections for the board of directors and officers of the company. It’s true, if you own only 10 shares your vote probably doesn’t count for much — but then the outcome of the vote probably doesn’t affect you as much either.

On the other hand, someone who owns 5% of all outstanding shares of a company has a lot more invested in the performance of that company than does someone who picked up 10 shares on the RobinHood App.

Of course, this is a bit subjective, but generally speaking, this is the idea.

## So Then What Is The Stock Market Exactly?

The term *stock market* actually refers to the entire collection of markets and exchanges around the world where the buying and selling of shares of companies take place.

There are many exchanges, too many to mention here… but don’t worry, let me make it simple:

The most famous stock exchange is the New York Stock Exchange (**NYSE**), or as it’s commonly affectionately called — **Wall Street**. While the NYSE is the oldest and most widely known of the exchanges, it is not actually the largest.

So which is the largest? The **Nasdaq**!

![](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/800/0*ImC-Ie5B4g7da30K.png)

Photo by Summer Sun on [Canva](https://www.canva.com/)

Unlike the NYSE, which has a physical address on Wall Street in New York City, the Nasdaq exists virtually and has no brick-and-mortar so to speak.

Now, while the management of Nasdaq is, indeed, based in NYC, Nasdaq is a global electronic marketplace, and so it doesn’t really *exist* anywhere.

Nasdaq was created in 1971 and was the world’s first electronic exchange, meaning that trading happened via computer rather than on pieces of paper inside a building somewhere — this is why most of the world’s tech giants, including Apple and Facebook, are listed on the Nasdaq.

The Nasdaq started out as a relatively obscure exchange, but as the tech industry flourished and became dominant, so too did the Nasdaq!

Another thing that the Nasdaq has that the NYSE does not is cryptocurrencies — more on that in another article.

**So here are the key takeaways:**

- The NYSE is old and stuffy and is primarily for large established firms.
- The Nasdaq is for fresh and techy companies and far more global in nature.
- The other major exchanges around the world include the FTSE in London, the Euronext in Europe, and the TYO (aka the NIKKEI) in, you guessed it, Tokyo.

Again, it’s important to remember that these exchanges have historically been for stocks only — The Nasdaq had broken rank by allowing cryptocurrencies.

In other words, stock exchanges do not trade currencies, commodities, or other types of financial **securities.** They do, however, allow for the trading of certain derivatives — more on that in future articles.

So when we say “Wall Street,” we mean the NYSE?

Yes, but no, but sort of yes. *Wall Street* has come to refer to the overall financial industry that manages investments, but at the center of it is the NYSE, which is where it all started — more on this in future articles as well.

## Putting It All Together

A stock exchange is where shares of companies are traded. Without stock exchanges, companies would not be able to offer shares of ownership to the public. Why is this important?

The reason why companies want to go public is because it can be an excellent way of raising money so that they can expand their operations — it can also be really great way to make the owners really super-wealthy, and quickly. However, going public can also be a great way to lose a lot of money fast and ultimately have to close your doors.

- Stock exchanges are themselves privately run organizations that are heavily regulated by governments.
- Just like any private organization, they flourish, merge, disband, or fail.
- The primary function of a stock exchange is to provide a marketplace for the trading company ownership.

When people who know what they are talking about say “The Stock Market,” they are referring to the overall collection of all the exchanges around the world that facilitate the trading of company shares.

Got it?
