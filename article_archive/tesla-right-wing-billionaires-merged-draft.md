Over the past three years, the world has seen a series of major supply chain disruptions caused by the pandemic, political tensions, climate change, natural disasters, and even shipping accidents. As a result, firms are increasingly keeping an eye on their balance sheets, since the next supply chain disruption might very well be the one that brings them down.

Supply Chain Finance (SCF) has, therefore, become a highly important and trending topic since it helps firms throughout the value chain attain funds earlier, thus they may have quicker access to working capital for financing future operations.

SCF is also the [best-known business practice for eliminating waste and excess since it shines a bright light on the total costs of both](https://www.paymentsjournal.com/supply-chain-finance-the-next-wave-of-business-growth/). If more firms implemented SCF methods in their business planning and operations, it would be good for both their balance sheet and for the planet.

In other words, SCF forces businesses to see the bigger picture and understand the full ecosystem that they exist within. Therefore, SCF causes businesses to make decisions based upon the sustainability of not only themselves but of every firm and customer that they depend upon. The result is that businesses begin to work with each other as partners, and are incentivized to help each other, rather than employing a ruthless set of competitive tactics aimed at improving the bottom line.

### Why are supply chain disruptions so destructive?

Anytime a manufacturer produces something, they must first purchase parts and materials from other firms, which are the suppliers. If one or more of those suppliers is delayed in delivering on time, then the entire chain gets held up, all the way down to the customer.

While this poses its own set of problems for the products directly affected, the real threat to many businesses is that this situation makes it impossible to produce other goods and services as well.

Why is that?

When a delay in the supply chain occurs, the money for facilitating that exchange is tied up with the delayed suppliers. In other words, if a manufacturer has already paid for components from a supplier, but there was a delay during delivery, then the manufacturer has to wait for that shipment to arrive before they can finish production, and subsequently sell their goods to generate more cash, which they then use to purchase components for other products.

Simply put, a supply chain disruption on one product can easily affect all products.

While there are a number of ways to take on debt for financing during these instances, all of these options come with a cost. For smaller firms, those costs can threaten the financial health of the business.

Remember when the [massive container ship from China, *Ever Given*, was wedged into the shoreline of the Suez Canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_Suez_Canal_obstruction), blocking it for six days?

The result was that hundreds of shipping vessels had to wait in line for more than a week. The alternative was to sail around the entire African Continent. During that episode, if you were a manufacturer with your cash tied up in components aboard one of those ships, then you likely lost a lot of money, or worse.

So how does Supply Chain Finance help prevent and resolve problems within the supply chain?

Simply put, by leveraging IT systems and business processes that facilitate quicker payment cycles, improved logistics planning, enhanced business relationships and contracts, favorable freight terms, and strong compliance programs, firms can greatly improve both the predictability and speed of their cash flow.

The result is enhanced working capital planning and financial management, which allows for actionable contingency plans during times of crisis and disruption.

Supply Chain Finance methods can be used to manage working capital during all the various growth phases of a firm, and to examine the many challenges and risks to firm sustainability. By properly understanding the financial impact of supplier and distributor relationships, inventory planning and purchasing, freight terms, business contracts, and payment cycles, firms of all shapes and sizes can become better partners for their suppliers, distributors, and customers.

### **So, what does Supply Chain Finance do exactly?**

The simplest way to explain it is that SCF looks for all the inefficiencies in a firm’s operations that cause the business to have less money to invest in its activities. The goal of SCF is to create strategic financial and operating plans that reduce financing costs for both buyers and sellers, enhance strategic partnerships throughout the value chain, and improve service levels to customers.

In other words, suppliers gain quicker access to cash to finance operations, buyers get more time to pay off their balances and reduce their costs, and distributors gain improved lines of credit. The result is happier customers, reduced waste, and healthier balance sheets at all nodes of the supply chain.

The key difference between an SCF approach and a traditional sales-focused market share approach is that SCF seeks to optimize working capital on the balance sheet, rather than maximize the number on the bottom line.

**Some examples of key business aspects that SCF seeks to analyze and improve are:**

- Supplier, distributor, and B2B customer contracts & agreements, for enhancing business relationships and partnerships.
- Selling customer and distributor invoices to financial firms to improve cash flow (invoice factoring).
- Reducing waste and eliminating excess and obsolete inventory.
- Bank loans vs. investor loans for improvements to logistics and inventory management.
- Costs, efficiency, and liability of freight and transportation terms.
- Demand Planning and operational compliance to improve working capital planning.
- Distribution contracts vs. operational compliance and inventory plans.
- Financing of key supplier operations via improved treasury management.
- Benefits and shortcomings of IT and Business Intelligence supply chain solutions (ERP, EDI, Data Management, BI, etc…).
- Supply chain fusion technology for automating receivables and payables management and improving cash flow.
- Streamlining cash flow, accelerating cash collection, and managing credits made available to distributors and B2B customers.

### **Powering the future**

Imagine that you run a medium-sized [LED light manufacturer](https://www.any-lamp.com/blog/led-reduced-co2-emissions-half-billion-tonnes) with a complex value chain that must be fully synchronized for you to stay competitive. The mission of your company is to [reduce energy consumption around the globe](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/led-lighting), and additionally, your product actually helps [reduce landfill waste and carbon emissions](https://www.any-lamp.com/blog/led-reduced-co2-emissions-half-billion-tonnes).

Several of your suppliers work closely with you to ensure your production floor gets the components and materials that you need on time, on budget, and at the quality that you demand. While you are technically the customer, your firm is just as dependent upon those suppliers as they are on you.

On the other side of the business are a number of distributors, all of various sizes and expertise. While one or two of your distributors depends upon your products to round out their product assortment, the rest have options for other suppliers of LED lights.

At the end of your value chain is your customer, who you depend upon to continue to purchase, use, and promote your product. However, the customer can only do this if they have access to the goods that you produce.

This is where the relationship with distributors becomes vital. Imagine for a moment that one of your distributors runs out of cash and cannot purchase enough inventory of your LED lights to meet the demand of your customers.

What do you do in that instance?

Distributors are typically strapped for cash since they work on thin margins, and are oftentimes sitting on excess inventory that they are waiting to return. In order to continue the flow of your business, you need to make sure that this distributor has enough working capital to procure your products.

Now, if you were smart and used SCF solutions to manage your supply chain, then you would be able to offer this key distributor payment terms where they don’t need to pay you within the normal cycle. It may sound simple, however, accomplishing this requires not only managing your own side of the street but also understanding well the operations and cash situations of both your suppliers and distributors.

Furthermore, many times issues like this exist not because of inefficiencies, but instead because of prioritization.

For example, if this distributor didn’t have the cash needed to pay quickly because it used it to purchase inventory from a different firm, then you could consider offering payment term incentives to encourage the distributor to pay you first before other suppliers.

Again, doing this comes at a cost, and understanding the cost vs. benefit of these options is what SCF does.

### Putting it all together

The major advantage of the Supply Chain Finance approach is that it is not focused exclusively on increasing margins on the P&L. If more firms implemented SCF methods, then they would be able to better partner with one another during times of crisis and disruption.

If businesses are incentivized to see the big picture and examine their ecosystem, then they will naturally adapt their strategy to a cooperative approach rather than an aggressively competitive one.

Supply Chain Finance might not be the only answer for sustainability in business, but it is certainly one of them.
